Colorado first time
home buyer programs

CHFA loans, down payment assistance and the rural programs most people on this corridor qualify for without knowing it. What each one is, and what it actually asks of you.

01 Start here

You probably need less money than you think

The single most common reason people on this corridor do not buy is that they believe they need twenty percent down. Almost nobody does, and out here there is a program most buyers qualify for that asks for nothing down at all.

Below is what exists, in plain language. I am a broker and not a lender, so treat this as the map rather than the advice: the numbers, limits and rules change, and your lender confirms what you personally qualify for. What I can tell you is which of these I see working here, and what they mean for the kind of house you can write on. When you are ready for the rest of it, the buying process covers the eight steps from first look to keys.

02 What exists

Six routes in, and most people qualify for more than one

These stack in some combinations and not others. A lender will tell you which pair is best for your situation, and the difference between a good pairing and a lazy one is often several thousand dollars at closing.

01

CHFA first mortgage loans

The Colorado Housing and Finance Authority runs several first mortgage programs, some restricted to first time buyers and qualified veterans, some open to repeat buyers. They carry income and purchase price limits that vary by county, and Garfield and Mesa limits are not the same.

02

CHFA down payment assistance

Paired with a CHFA first mortgage, this is the piece that actually closes the gap. It comes as either a grant or a second mortgage depending on the program you choose. This is the part people do not know exists.

03

USDA rural development

Zero down, and most of this corridor is inside an eligible area. Rifle, Silt, New Castle, Parachute, Meeker and De Beque are the kind of places this was written for. Income limits apply and they are more generous than people expect.

04

VA loans

Zero down with no mortgage insurance for eligible service members and veterans, and it can be used more than once. If you have service history, start here before anything else.

05

FHA

A low down payment and more forgiving credit requirements. Worth knowing that FHA appraisals are stricter on rural property: a well, a septic field or a private road can all raise conditions a conventional appraiser would not.

06

Conventional low down payment

Several conventional products start at three percent down for first time buyers. Often the cheapest option overall if your credit is strong, because the mortgage insurance falls away later.

Most of this corridor is a USDA eligible area

That means zero down on a normal house in a normal town, and it is the single most underused program here.

03 What it takes

Four things to do before you apply

01

Take the homebuyer education course

CHFA requires it for their programs and it is genuinely useful. It is online, it takes an evening, and finishing it early means it is never the thing holding up your file.

02

Talk to a lender who has done these here

CHFA and USDA both have paperwork that a lender unfamiliar with them will get wrong or avoid offering. Ask directly how many they closed last year. I can point you at people who do this every month.

03

Check the income and price limits for your county

They differ between Garfield, Mesa and Rio Blanco, and they change. Being over a limit by a small margin sometimes just means choosing a different program rather than giving up.

04

Find out what the house itself has to pass

This is the step people skip. A well, a septic system or a shared driveway can fail a program's requirements even when the buyer qualifies easily, and out here that is most houses over an acre.

04 Questions first time buyers ask

The ones that come up every time

Am I still a first time buyer if I owned a house years ago?

Often yes. Most programs define it as not having owned a home in the last three years rather than never having owned one. Qualified veterans are frequently exempt from the requirement entirely. Worth asking rather than assuming you are out.

Does down payment assistance have to be repaid?

It depends which form you take. Some comes as a grant that does not, some as a second mortgage that does. The trade is usually a slightly different rate on the first mortgage. Your lender will show you both side by side, and the right answer depends on how long you plan to stay.

Can I use these on a house with a well and septic?

Usually, but the property has to pass the program's own requirements, which are stricter than a standard purchase. Expect a water test and a septic inspection to be required rather than optional. The buying guide covers what those look for.

What credit score do I need?

Lower than most people assume, and different for each program. A lender can tell you in one phone call, free, without a hard credit pull in many cases. If you are close to a threshold there are usually quick fixes that move you over it.

Is it worth it if I can afford a normal down payment?

Sometimes. Keeping cash for the things that come after closing, like a well pump or a new heat source, is often worth more than a larger down payment. That is a real conversation to have rather than a default.

Where do I actually start?

The lender, before the house. Everything else follows from what they tell you, including which towns are realistic. Call me and I will tell you who to speak to, and while you wait for their answer, read up on the towns that fit the number.
