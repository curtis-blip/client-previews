Buyer's Guide

Buying a Home in Sarasota, FL

Eight steps from the pre-approval to the keys, the Florida way, whether you live here now or are moving to Sarasota from out of state.

How it works

Buying in Florida, From Out of State or Down the Street

A Florida purchase has its own sequence: a written agreement with your agent's brokerage, a contract full of deadlines, inspections your insurer will want to see, and a closing you may be able to sign from home. The steps below take them in turn.

Ryan and Katie made this move themselves, and many of their clients buy from out of state. Katie's reviews on Zillow describe Zoom walk-throughs, answers on weekends and one family who bought sight unseen.

Already living on the Suncoast? The steps are the same, and you get to tour in person.

Step 1 of 8

Get Pre-Approved Before You Tour

A lender checks your income, savings, debts and credit and puts in writing the amount it would lend you. A preapproval letter is not a guaranteed loan offer, so keep your finances steady until closing.

Get Loan Estimates from two or three lenders and compare the costs side by side. With the letter in hand before you tour, you can write an offer the day you find the house.

Step 2 of 8

Pick the Town, Then the House

Twelve towns across Sarasota and Manatee counties live very differently. Siesta Key, Anna Maria Island and Longboat Key are islands on the Gulf; downtown Sarasota and Bradenton sit on the water; Lakewood Ranch and Wellen Park are master-planned communities.

Ryan and Katie start with how you want to spend an ordinary weekday, then help you narrow the twelve to the two or three that fit. The community pages and the Moving to Sarasota guide cover each town.

03

Step 3 of 8

Tour Homes in Person or on Video

Before you tour, you sign a written buyer agreement with Ryan and Katie's brokerage. Buyer's agent compensation is negotiable and set in your written buyer agreement. Sometimes the seller agrees to pay part or all of it. Florida law presumes your agent works as a transaction broker unless you agree to another relationship in writing.

Can't be here? Ryan and Katie walk the home with you on a video call; Katie's reviews mention Zoom walk-throughs and answers on weekends.

Sources for Step 3

04

Step 4 of 8

Write an Offer on Florida's Terms

Once the seller signs your offer, it is the contract. The price, the deposit, the financing, the inspection period and the closing date are all in it, along with every deadline, so read them before you sign.

Florida also puts a few notices in front of you at or before the contract: a property tax disclosure that warns your taxes will not match the seller's, a radon gas notice, and, where they apply, the homeowners association's disclosure summary and the community development district's notice of its own taxes.

Sources for Step 4

In Florida, the seller tells you in writing what they know about past flooding.

05

Step 5 of 8

Inspect the House, Then the Roof and Windows

Hire a licensed home inspector during your inspection period. Florida licenses home inspectors through the Department of Business and Professional Regulation, and you can look one up by name.

Two shorter reports matter for insurance here. A wind mitigation inspection documents the roof, the windows and the shutters, and Florida requires insurers to offer discounts for qualifying features. An insurer may also ask for a four-point inspection of the roof, the electrical, the plumbing and the heating and air; Citizens requires one for homes more than 20 years old.

Sources for Step 5

06

Step 6 of 8

Get Insurance Quotes and Check the Flood Zone

Budget for homeowners insurance before anything else, and get quotes on the actual house during your inspection period, not the week before closing. The roof's age, the wind features, the elevation and the flood zone all move the price.

Most homeowners policies do not cover flood, and in a high-risk flood area your lender may require a separate flood policy. Look the address up on FEMA's flood maps, and read the seller's flood disclosure. If private insurers turn a home down, Citizens Property Insurance, created by the Florida Legislature, covers owners who cannot find insurance in the private market.

Sources for Step 6

07

Step 7 of 8

Close From Wherever You Are

Your contract names who closes the sale, often a title company or a real estate attorney. Your lender has to give you the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing; check it against your Loan Estimate. An owner's title policy is optional; it covers you if someone later claims a right to the home from before your purchase, such as a previous owner's unpaid taxes.

A Florida online notary can notarize your signature over a video call wherever you are, so many buyers sign without the trip; your lender and the closing agent decide if your closing can be signed that way. Wiring money? Confirm the instructions by phone with the closing agent, at a number you saved at the start. Scammers send fake instructions by email.

Sources for Step 7

08

Step 8 of 8

Move In, Then File for Homestead

Once Florida is your permanent home, you have 30 days to get a Florida driver license and 10 days to title and register your car here, counted from when you establish residency.

If you own the home and live in it as your permanent residence on January 1, apply to the county property appraiser for the homestead exemption by March 1. Then look up your evacuation level and make a plan before June 1, when hurricane season begins.

Questions

Questions Buyers Ask About Buying in Florida

Can I buy a home here before I move?

Yes. Ryan and Katie tour homes with you on video, and a Florida online notary can notarize your signature over a video call wherever you are. Your lender and the closing agent decide if your closing can be signed that way.

  • Online notarization, Florida Statutes section 117.209
Why will my property taxes be higher than the seller's?

A sale resets the assessment. The property appraiser reassesses the home at its just value on January 1 after you buy, and the seller's homestead exemption and Save Our Homes benefit do not pass to you. Florida contracts warn you about this in the property tax disclosure.

  • Property taxes for first-time Florida homebuyers, from the Florida Department of Revenue
  • Property tax disclosure, Florida Statutes section 689.261
Do I need flood insurance?

Most homeowners policies do not cover flood. In a high-risk flood area your lender may require a flood policy, and outside one the choice is yours. Check the address on FEMA's flood maps and ask for a quote early.

  • Flood insurance basics, from FEMA's FloodSmart
  • Flood maps by address, from FEMA's Map Service Center
What is a CDD, and why is it on my tax bill?

A community development district is a special district that pays to build, run and maintain certain public facilities and services where it sits. Its board sets its taxes and assessments each year, they come on top of your county taxes, and Florida requires a notice in bold type in your contract that says so.

  • Community development district notice, Florida Statutes section 190.048
How does paying a buyer's agent work?

Buyer's agent compensation is negotiable and set in your written buyer agreement. Sometimes the seller agrees to pay part or all of it. The agreement spells out what you will pay and how.

  • Written buyer agreements, from the National Association of REALTORS
I already own a homestead in Florida. Can I bring the tax savings with me?

Possibly. Portability lets most Florida homestead owners transfer all or part of their Save Our Homes benefit to a new Florida homestead, up to $500,000, if they establish the new one within three years of January 1 of the year they left the old one. File the transfer form with your homestead application by March 1.

  • Save Our Homes and portability, from the Florida Department of Revenue
  • Portability, from the Manatee County Property Appraiser (2026)
