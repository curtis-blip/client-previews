Land and claims

Land and mining claims in Gilpin County

Vacant lots, acreage, patented and unpatented claims. Most of what I have sold recently is land, and it is the hardest thing in this county to price correctly.

What is mine worth
Why this page exists

Land does not behave like a house

A house has neighbours that sold last quarter. A twelve acre parcel on an unmaintained road with an unpermitted well has none, and that single fact changes how it is priced, financed, insured and marketed.

No comparables, by default

Acreage, slope, exposure, access and utilities vary so much parcel to parcel that automated valuation has nothing useful to work with. Somebody has to build the analysis by hand.

Financing is a different animal

Most residential lenders will not write on raw land at all. The ones who do want more down, shorter terms and a clear picture of access and water.

The title work is the deal

Old claims, severed mineral rights, unrecorded easements and access that exists in practice but not on paper. This is where land transactions succeed or collapse.

Claims

Patented, unpatented, and why the difference matters

Gilpin County has been mining country since 1859, so claims turn up constantly in this market. The terminology gets used loosely, and the distinction is the first thing to establish.

Generally

Patented claim

  • Title passed into private ownership historically
  • Held and conveyed as private real property
  • Subject to county taxes like other private land
  • Mineral rights may still have been severed at some point
  • Whether it can be built on depends on county zoning and access
Generally

Unpatented claim

  • A mineral right on federal land, not ownership of the surface
  • Depends on annual filings and fees staying current
  • Administered through the Bureau of Land Management
  • Surface use is limited to what supports the mineral activity
  • Not a residential building lot, whatever a listing implies
Either way

What to verify

  • Current status and filing history, at the source
  • Whether minerals are severed from the surface
  • Legal access, recorded rather than habitual
  • What the county will actually permit
  • Any reclamation or environmental obligation

The descriptions above are general orientation, not a determination about any specific parcel or claim, and not legal advice. Claim status, mineral ownership and access all have to be confirmed through title work, the county record and the BLM before you rely on them. On any claim transaction I will tell you plainly which questions are still open.

Buying land

The five questions that decide whether a parcel is worth anything

  • Can you get water, legally

    A well needs a permit from the Division of Water Resources, and the permit type controls what you may use the water for. On raw land this is question one, not question five.

  • Will it take a septic system

    Soil and slope decide it, and the county wants a percolation test. A parcel that will not perc is a very different asset from one that will.

  • Is the access recorded

    Plenty of parcels here are reached by a road that everybody uses and nobody has an easement for. Habitual access is not legal access and lenders know the difference.

  • What will the county permit

    Zoning, setbacks, and whether a building permit is realistic at all. Worth establishing before you buy a view you cannot build on.

  • Who owns the minerals

    Surface and mineral estates were separated all over this county a century ago. It affects value, and occasionally it affects what can happen on the surface.

Selling land

How I price and market a parcel

01

Establish what you actually have

Acreage, access, water and waste feasibility, mineral ownership, and what the county would permit. Half the parcels I look at are worth more than the owner thought and the other half are worth less, and both cases come from these answers.

02

Build comparables that survive scrutiny

Widen the search deliberately, adjust for the things that differ, and document every adjustment. That analysis has to hold up to a buyer, a lender and eventually an appraiser with the same data problem.

03

Answer the questions in the listing

Most land listings in this corridor say acreage and price and leave a buyer guessing about water, access and permits. Guessing buyers do not drive up from Denver. Answering them is most of the marketing.

04

Reach the buyers who exist

The pool for a mountain parcel is small, specific and mostly in the metro. Syndication is automatic, calling the agents whose buyers match is not, and on land it is where the showings come from.
