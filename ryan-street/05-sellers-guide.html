Selling a Home in North County San Diego

The eight steps of a California sale, from the first price to the recorded deed, with an agent whose sales are mostly listings.

How it works

How a Sale Works, From the First Price to the Deed

A sale here runs on California's rules: a written listing agreement, the disclosures the state requires, a purchase contract with its own time periods and a deed recorded with the county. The eight steps below follow that order, and each rule links to the office that sets it.

More of Ryan's sales are in Escondido than in any other town, and he works from an office on Grand Avenue there. His brokerage is LPT Realty, Inc., and your listing goes into CRMLS, the California Regional MLS.

Temecula, Murrieta and Menifee are in Riverside County and the other seven towns are in San Diego County, so the deed is recorded with whichever county the home is in.

Start With an Estimate, Then a Price

Type your address into the home value estimator for an instant estimate: a value, a likely range and a confidence score. It is a starting point, worked out without anyone seeing inside your house.

02

Step 2 of 8

Sign the Listing Agreement

The listing agreement authorizes Ryan's brokerage, LPT Realty, to market and sell your home. It sets the list price, how long the listing runs and the commission.

Commission is negotiable and set in the listing agreement. Read the whole agreement before you sign, including when the listing ends.

03

Step 3 of 8

Prepare the House

Fix what a buyer's inspector would flag, clear the counters and closets, and plan the photographs for the best light of the day.

California also requires a working smoke alarm in a single-family home that is sold, carbon monoxide alarms where the home has a fuel-burning appliance, a fireplace or an attached garage, and a water heater braced against earthquakes. You confirm the smoke alarm and the water heater in writing.

04

Step 4 of 8

Go Live on CRMLS

Once the photographs are done, the listing goes into CRMLS, the California Regional MLS, where the agents who belong to it can see it and show it to their buyers.

Through IDX Broker, the listing also appears on this site's Home Search and on his listings page, with the photographs, the price and the facts buyers search by.

Most of Ryan's sales are listings, where he represents the owner.

05

Step 5 of 8

Fill Out the Seller's Disclosures

California requires a Transfer Disclosure Statement on the sale of a home, with some exceptions (Civil Code section 1102): your answers on its condition, from what you know. If the contract calls for a Seller Property Questionnaire, a California Association of REALTORS form that asks more, you answer it from your actual knowledge too.

The Natural Hazard Disclosure Statement covers flood, dam inundation, fire hazard, wildland, earthquake fault and seismic hazard zones, and a home built before 1978 needs the federal lead-based paint disclosure.

06

Step 6 of 8

Review Offers and Negotiate

Offers come in from buyers' agents, and you decide on each one: accept, counter or decline. Look past the price to the terms: the deposit, the loan, the contingencies and their time periods, and the closing date. Negotiating is the part of the job Ryan describes in his own words:

07

Step 7 of 8

Escrow, Inspections and the Appraisal

Escrow opens once you accept, with the buyer's deposit. In Southern California the escrow holder is most often an independent company licensed by the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation, and it holds the money and the documents until every condition is met.

The buyer's inspectors come through in the time the contract allows, and any requests for repairs or credits that follow are negotiated with you. If the buyer is borrowing, the lender's appraiser visits too.

08

Step 8 of 8

Closing and Recording

You sign the grant deed in front of a notary, because California records a deed only once its signing is acknowledged (Government Code section 27287). The escrow holder pays off your mortgage and the costs of the sale from the buyer's funds.

The sale closes when the deed is recorded, with the San Diego County Recorder or with Riverside County, and your proceeds are sent to you. Confirm wiring instructions for them by phone, as the Department of Real Estate advises.

Inherited homes and rentals

Inherited the House, or Selling a Rental?

I inherited the house. Does it go through probate?

Not always. Property with a named beneficiary or held jointly with survivorship rights can pass without a probate case. If the owner died on or after April 1, 2025 and the house was their main home, worth $750,000 or less, you can ask the court to transfer it without the full probate process. When the estate does go through probate, a judge appoints a personal representative to act for it.

  • How probate works, from the California Courts
  • When formal probate may not be needed, from the California Courts
Can I sell a rental and put off the tax?

If the sale is part of an exchange for other real estate you will hold for business or as an investment, Section 1031 generally means the gain is not taxed at the time of the exchange. You are taxed on the gain up to the amount of any cash or other property you receive, a home held mainly to resell does not qualify, and the exchange is reported on Form 8824. Your tax adviser can tell you the deadlines.

  • Like-kind exchanges, from the IRS
The house is in a trust. Who signs?

The trustee does, under the trust's own terms. A trust avoids probate on property titled in the trust's name, so check the deed: a house that was never put into the trust can still need probate. An attorney can advise on the steps.

  • Wills, estates and trusts, from the San Diego Superior Court
The buyer has a VA loan. What changes for me?

Mostly the appraisal. Every VA purchase contract has to include the VA escape clause. If your price is higher than the reasonable value VA sets for the home, the clause lets the buyer back out without losing their earnest money deposit. The buyer can also go ahead and cover the difference with a down payment.

  • The VA escape clause, from the Department of Veterans Affairs
I've already moved away. Can I still sell?

Yes, and you do not have to come back to sign. The grant deed is signed in front of a notary, and a notary where you live now can take that signature: California accepts an acknowledgment taken in another place under that place's own law.

  • Civil Code section 1189

Questions

What Sellers Ask

How is the commission set?

Commission is negotiable and set in the listing agreement. California requires listing agreements for homes of one to four units to state that the amount or rate of commission is not fixed by law and is set by each broker individually (Business and Professions Code section 10147.5).

How accurate is the instant estimate?

It is a starting point. The estimator returns a value, a likely range and a confidence score from your address, but it cannot see inside your house. When you want a list price, ask Ryan for a market analysis.

Can I sell my home as is?

You can offer it as is, but the disclosures still apply. California's Legislature made clear that the Transfer Disclosure Statement may not be waived in an as-is sale (Civil Code section 1102.1), and the buyer can still inspect.

What happens if the appraisal comes in low?

The CFPB tells buyers they can often use a low appraisal to negotiate the price down, and that, depending on the contract, they may cancel. That usually reopens the price, or ends in a cancellation the contract allows.

Can I sell if I still owe on my mortgage?

Yes. The Department of Real Estate's escrow brochure lists your mortgage account information among the items you give the escrow officer, and the loan is paid off from the sale at closing, before your proceeds are sent to you.

Will tax be withheld from my proceeds?

California treats real estate withholding as a prepayment of state income tax. The Franchise Tax Board's Form 593 lists the exemptions, and you file it with escrow before closing. Federal tax on the sale of a main home is covered in IRS Publication 523. Your tax adviser can tell you what applies to you.
