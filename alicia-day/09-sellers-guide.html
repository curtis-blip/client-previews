How to sell your house

What buyers in these ten counties will actually pay, what to fix before listing, and what to leave alone.

01 / The hard part

Pricing is the whole game

Almost every problem in a slow sale traces back to the opening price. A house priced correctly draws its best offers in the first two or three weeks, while it is new and everyone waiting on the market is looking at it. A house priced above what the comparables support burns through that window, then sits, and a house that has sat gets treated as damaged goods regardless of why.

Reducing later rarely recovers it. The buyers who would have paid full price in week one have moved on, and the ones left are watching for further cuts.

03 / Fix or leave

Where the money comes back

Fix: the roof

A roof at end of life will come up in every inspection and every lender conversation. It cannot be avoided.

Fix: paint and trim

The cheapest return in the business. Neutral, clean, everywhere.

Fix: the front

Clear the beds, cut the grass, wash the porch. Buyers form the price in their head from the driveway.

Leave: the kitchen

A full renovation almost never returns its cost here. Clean and functional beats new and expensive.

Leave: the flooring

Unless it is damaged. Buyers change flooring to their own taste anyway.

Leave: the pool

Adding one before selling is money you will not get back in this market.

04 / Before you list

Start with the number

A valuation costs nothing and commits you to nothing, and it is the only sensible first step.

What is my home worth
Questions

Asked before every closing

The honest answer is a range, not a figure, and it depends on condition, land, and what has genuinely sold nearby rather than what the county average says. The online estimates are unreliable in rural markets because there is not enough comparable sales data for the models to work from. A walkthrough gets you a real range and the reasoning behind it.

It varies sharply by town. Hinesville turns over faster on Fort Stewart rotations. Smaller markets can take a season. Correctly priced, most houses here draw their strongest interest in the first two to three weeks. If a house is still sitting after a month, the price is nearly always the reason.

Usually not. Big-ticket renovations rarely return their cost. What does return reliably is paint, cleaning, decluttering, tidy landscaping and fixing anything actively broken. Ask before you spend, because the list of things worth doing is shorter than most people assume.

Agent commission, some closing costs, and any repairs you agree to after the inspection. In a slower market sellers sometimes contribute toward the buyer's closing costs as well. You should get an estimated net sheet before you list, so the figure at the end is not a surprise.

It depends on your equity and your tolerance for moving twice. Selling first is financially cleaner and gives you a stronger position as a buyer. Buying first is more comfortable but usually needs bridge financing or a contingent offer, which is weaker in a competitive situation. Worth mapping out properly before you commit either way.

Then something is wrong with price, condition or presentation, and it is nearly always price. The useful response is to look at the showing feedback and the competing listings honestly, rather than waiting longer and hoping.
