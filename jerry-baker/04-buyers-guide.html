Buyer's guide

Buying a home in Gilpin County

A mountain purchase turns on questions a Denver contract never asks. Here is how I work through them with you, in order, before you are committed to anything.

Why me

I spent twenty years pricing property nobody had comparables for

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development recruited me in 1988 to sell its single family and multifamily portfolios. Two decades of that is unusual training for a mountain market, and it is exactly the right training.

I answer my own phone

A two person office means nothing gets handed to an assistant you have not met. You get me from the first call to the closing table.

Diligence before the offer

Well permits, septic condition, road maintenance and insurance get checked while you can still walk away, not during a rushed inspection window.

Negotiation as a discipline

Master Certified Negotiation Expert, designated by Oliver Frascona. On a thin market, the negotiation is most of the outcome.

The process

Eight steps, in the order they actually happen

Photo: lender meeting or paperwork

Find a lender who closes up here

Plenty of lenders will pre-approve you and then withdraw when they see raw land, a non-conforming cabin or an off-grid system. I keep a short list of the ones who write that paper regularly. Starting here saves the most time of anything in this process.

Photo: Jerry with clients, map or plat

Work out what you actually want

Year round access or summer only. Well or hauled water. A commute to Denver or no commute at all. These decisions narrow the map faster than a price range does, and getting them wrong is what makes people buy twice.

Set up the search properly

I build you a saved search for the specific towns and property types that fit, so new listings reach you the morning they hit the MLS. Up here inventory is thin enough that a day matters.

Photo: property walkthrough, exterior

Walk it in person, in the worst weather you can

A property that shows beautifully in July can be unreachable in February. I would rather drive you up a bad road once than explain it to you afterwards. Bring boots.

Photo: well head, septic or utility detail

Diligence: water, waste, access, insurance

I pull the well permit from the Division of Water Resources, check whether the septic has a current use permit, find out who maintains the road, and confirm a carrier will insure it. Four answers that decide whether the rest of this matters.

Photo: contract signing or desk detail

Write the offer

Price is one term of many. On mountain property the contingencies, the closing timeline and who carries the cost of a failed system are often worth more than the number at the top.

Photo: inspection in progress

Inspection, appraisal, insurance binder

Three things run in parallel and any of them can move the deal. Appraisals are the usual problem when comparables are scarce, which is where twenty years of portfolio work earns its keep.

Photo: keys, front door or closing

Close, and know what you own

You leave the table with the permits, the maintenance contacts and the service records, not just a key. Most of my referrals come from what happens after closing rather than before it.

Diligence

What diligence means at nine thousand feet

In the metro, diligence is an inspection and a title commitment. Up here it is a different list, and every item on it has ended a transaction for somebody who did not check.

  • Permit is not the same as right

    A household-use-only well permit will not let you irrigate or water stock. Two different documents, two different answers.

  • Septic transfers with conditions

    Gilpin County wants a use permit and an inspection at transfer. A failed system is a real number, not a talking point.

  • Somebody plows, or nobody does

    County maintained, private association, or no arrangement at all. The third case is common and it is worth knowing before you own it.

  • Insurance now has conditions

    Carriers want defensible space and roofing detail, sometimes an inspection, before they will quote at all. Start it early.
