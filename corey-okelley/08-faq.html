Commercial Real Estate FAQ

COMMERCIAL REAL ESTATE questions, answered.

Plain answers to the questions buyers, sellers, tenants, and investors ask most, on leases, valuation, cap rates, and how commercial deals actually work on the Gulf Coast.

Commercial real estate moves on its own vocabulary, and the terms matter because they drive price, risk, and return. Below are the questions Corey hears most often from owners and occupiers across Pensacola and Northwest Florida. If you have a question that is not covered here, reach out and Corey will walk you through it.

A triple net lease, often written as NNN, is a commercial lease where the tenant pays a base rent plus the three major operating costs of the property: real estate taxes, building insurance, and maintenance (including common area maintenance, or CAM). Because those expenses pass through to the tenant, the landlord receives a more predictable net income with fewer surprises. NNN structures are common in retail and single-tenant net lease properties, and they are a core reason investors favor those assets. For the full mechanics, Investopedia has a clear breakdown of the triple net (NNN) lease.

Net operating income, or NOI, is the income a property produces after operating expenses but before debt payments and income taxes. You calculate it by taking gross rental income plus other property income, then subtracting operating costs such as property taxes, insurance, management, utilities, and maintenance. NOI is the single most important number in commercial valuation because cap rates and most pricing models are built directly on it. Corey normalizes income to a true NOI when he values a property, so the number reflects sustainable performance rather than a single good or bad year.

A capitalization rate, or cap rate, is the property’s net operating income divided by its price or value, expressed as a percentage. If a property produces $100,000 in NOI and is worth $1.25 million, the cap rate is 8 percent. A higher cap rate generally signals higher return and higher perceived risk, while a lower cap rate reflects a premium, lower-risk asset. Cap rates vary by asset class, tenant quality, lease term, and submarket, which is why local market knowledge matters when you read one. You can see how Corey applies cap rate and NOI analysis on the investment property page.

A Broker Opinion of Value, or BOV, is a broker’s informed estimate of what a commercial property is worth, based on its income, comparable sales, and prevailing cap rates for its asset class. It gives owners a clear, defensible value range to guide pricing, refinancing, or planning decisions, usually within a few business days and at no cost. Corey reviews the rent roll, operating expenses, and lease structures, normalizes income to NOI, and weighs the result against current sales comps and market conditions. You can request a free BOV for your property anytime.

A BOV is a broker’s professional opinion of value, typically free and delivered in a few business days, intended to guide a pricing or strategy decision. A formal appraisal is a certified report prepared by a licensed appraiser, which can cost thousands of dollars and take weeks, and is usually required by lenders or for legal and tax matters. The two serve different purposes: a BOV helps you move quickly and decide whether to act, while an appraisal carries the formal weight a bank or court needs. Buying commercial property starts with defining your objective, whether you intend to occupy the space, hold it as an investment, or develop it, since that shapes everything that follows. From there you set a budget and financing plan, identify target submarkets, and evaluate properties on income, condition, zoning, and lease structure before making an offer. Due diligence is critical: you verify financials, leases, title, environmental conditions, and any deferred maintenance during the contract period. Corey represents buyers and owner-users from site selection through closing, including off-market opportunities. See commercial property for current and sourced listings.

Leasing commercial space begins with a clear needs assessment: how much square footage you require, the layout and visibility your business needs, your budget, and your ideal location and timeline. Corey then identifies matching retail or office space, helps you compare options, and negotiates the lease terms, including base rent, the expense structure (such as NNN versus gross), escalations, tenant improvement allowances, and renewal options. Reading those terms correctly is where good representation pays for itself, because the headline rent is rarely the whole cost. Explore available commercial property or tell Corey your criteria.

Tenant representation means an advisor works exclusively on the tenant’s side of a lease, rather than for the landlord. The advisor handles site selection, market analysis, and lease negotiation entirely in the occupier’s interest, working to secure favorable rent, concessions, and flexibility. Because the landlord typically pays the commission, tenant representation usually costs the tenant nothing while leveling a negotiation that would otherwise favor the property owner. Corey represents tenants and buyers as part of his full-service advisory services.

A 1031 exchange, named for Section 1031 of the U.S. tax code, lets an investor defer capital gains taxes when selling an investment property by reinvesting the proceeds into another like-kind property. The rules are strict on timing: you generally must identify a replacement property within 45 days of the sale and close on it within 180 days, using a qualified intermediary to hold the funds. Done correctly, it lets investors trade up or reposition a portfolio without an immediate tax hit, which can meaningfully compound returns over time. Always confirm the details with your tax advisor, and see investment property for how Corey helps investors plan acquisitions and dispositions.

Commercial real estate is valued primarily on the income it produces, while residential value leans on comparable sales and emotional appeal. Commercial leases are longer and more complex, financing terms and down payments are different, and deals involve more due diligence around zoning, leases, and tenant credit. The metrics differ too: commercial decisions turn on cap rate, NOI, and price per square foot rather than price per bedroom.
