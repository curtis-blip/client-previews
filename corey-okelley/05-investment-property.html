Investment Advisory

Underwritten before it is recommended.

Commercial real estate investment is fundamentally a numbers exercise, and that is where Corey O’Kelley starts. Every income-producing retail or office asset is evaluated on what it actually earns: the cap rate, the net operating income, the strength and term of the tenancy, and the price per square foot measured against real comparable sales. The goal is a property that performs, not just one that closes.

Corey advises investors across the full life of an asset, from acquisition through disposition. On the buy side that means sourcing opportunities, running the underwriting, and pressure-testing the assumptions before you commit capital. On the sell side it means positioning the asset, marketing it to qualified investors, and negotiating terms that protect your return. Many of the best opportunities are quiet ones, off-market deals shared directly with investors who have clear criteria on file.

Beyond the local Pensacola and Gulf Coast market, investors also search national listing channels and investment marketplaces like Crexi. Corey works alongside those channels with something they cannot offer on their own: a local advisor who knows the submarkets, reads the leases, and tells you when a deal does not pencil. The discipline is the same whether the property is single-tenant net lease or a multi-tenant strip center, analyze the income, weigh the risk, and recommend only what holds up to scrutiny.

The Investor Lens

How Corey reads an investment.

Three questions decide whether an income-producing property is worth your capital. Corey works through each one before a recommendation is ever made.

01

Cap rate & NOI

The analysis begins with the income. Corey normalizes the rent roll and operating expenses to a true net operating income, then measures it against the price as a capitalization rate, so you can compare the return to the market and to other opportunities on an honest, apples-to-apples basis.

02

Tenancy & lease term

Income is only as durable as the tenant behind it. Corey examines who occupies the space, how strong the tenant is, how much term remains on the lease, and how it is structured, NNN versus gross, escalations, and renewal options, because the lease is what carries the asset through the hold.

03

Price per SF & comps

Finally, the price is weighed against the market. Corey benchmarks price per square foot against recent comparable sales and prevailing cap rates for the asset class and submarket, so you know whether you are paying a fair number, and where the upside or the risk really sits.

Questions, Answered

Commercial investment FAQ.

A capitalization rate, or cap rate, is a property’s net operating income divided by its purchase price, expressed as a percentage. It is the standard way to gauge the unleveraged return on a commercial investment and to compare one asset against another. A lower cap rate generally signals a lower-risk, higher-priced asset, while a higher cap rate often reflects more risk or more upside.

Net operating income, or NOI, is the income a property generates after operating expenses but before debt service and income taxes. It is calculated by subtracting operating costs, things like taxes, insurance, maintenance, and management, from gross rental income. NOI is the foundation of nearly every commercial valuation, including the cap rate and the Broker Opinion of Value.

In a triple net (NNN) lease, the tenant pays base rent plus the property’s taxes, insurance, and maintenance, which gives the owner more predictable net income. In a gross lease, the landlord covers most or all of those operating expenses out of the rent collected. NNN structures are common in single-tenant net-lease investments, while gross or modified-gross leases are more typical in multi-tenant office.

A 1031 exchange is a provision in the U.S. tax code that lets an investor defer capital gains tax by reinvesting the proceeds from the sale of one investment property into another like-kind property within set timeframes. It is a common tool for moving equity into a larger or better-positioned asset without an immediate tax hit. Corey can help structure the real estate side of an exchange, and you should always confirm the tax details with your own advisor.

A strong commercial investment combines durable income with manageable risk: a creditworthy tenant, meaningful term remaining on a well-structured lease, a fair price per square foot relative to comps, and a location with staying power. The right answer depends on your goals, whether you are after steady net-lease income or value-add upside.
