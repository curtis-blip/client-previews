WHAT IS YOUR COMMERCIAL PROPERTY worth?

A Broker Opinion of Value gives you a clear, market-grounded estimate of what your retail, office, or investment property is worth, drawn from real income, comparable sales, and current cap rates. Free, confidential, and without the cost or delay of a formal appraisal.

What It Is

A Broker Opinion of Value, explained.

A Broker Opinion of Value, often shortened to BOV, is a broker’s informed estimate of what a commercial property would realistically sell or lease for in the current market. It is the fastest, most practical way to answer a simple question: what is my property worth today? For most owners weighing a sale, a refinance, or a long-term hold, a BOV gives you the number you need to make a confident decision, without committing to the time and expense of a certified valuation.

Corey O’Kelley prepares each BOV personally, grounded in more than 20 years of specialty finance experience and a deep working knowledge of the Pensacola and Gulf Coast commercial market. This is not an automated estimate or a number pulled from a website. It is a considered opinion of value, built from the same financial discipline he applies to every transaction.

BOV versus a formal appraisal

A BOV and a formal appraisal answer similar questions, but they are very different instruments. A BOV is a broker’s opinion: it is fast, it is free, and it is intended to guide pricing, negotiation, and strategy. A formal appraisal is a certified report prepared by a licensed appraiser to a regulated standard. It carries more formal weight, but it can cost thousands of dollars and take weeks to complete, which is why lenders and courts require one while most owners and investors do not. If your goal is to understand the market and price a property correctly, a BOV is usually the right tool. If a lender, attorney, or tax authority specifically requires a certified appraisal, Corey will tell you so and point you in the right direction.

How Corey arrives at a value

A defensible value range comes from analysis, not guesswork. For income-producing property, Corey reviews the rent roll, operating expenses, and lease structures, including how common area maintenance, or CAM, and other recoveries are handled, then normalizes that income to a true net operating income, or NOI. He evaluates that NOI against recent sales comparables and the prevailing cap rates for the asset class and submarket, and weighs the property’s replacement cost and competitive positioning. Where the situation calls for it, he applies an income-capitalization approach and, for more complex assets, a discounted cash flow analysis. The result is a clear, defensible value range you can actually use, not a single number you have to take on faith.
